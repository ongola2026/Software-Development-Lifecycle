## Reflection Questions

### How do I interpret user needs and implement them into a program? How does creating "user stories" help with this?

I have come to see interpreting user needs as an active, ongoing process rather than a one-time requirements-gathering step. Early in this course, I sat in the Product Owner role and had to translate a focus group's scattered feedback, things like wanting a personalized destination list instead of a generic one, or wanting to filter trips by vacation type, into something a development team could actually build. User stories were the tool that made that translation possible. Writing each need as "As a [user], I want to [task], so that I can [goal]" forced me to keep the *why* attached to every feature, not just the *what*. That mattered later in the course when I moved into the Developer and Tester roles: having a clear goal statement made it much easier to write accurate acceptance criteria and test cases, and to catch ambiguity before it became a costly misunderstanding. I also learned that user stories aren't static. When the Product Owner in our SNHU Travel scenario pivoted the project toward detox/wellness travel destinations partway through, having the backlog structured as small, independent stories meant that pivot only required updating a handful of stories, not rebuilding the whole plan from scratch.

### How do I approach developing programs? What Agile processes do I hope to incorporate into my future development work?

My approach to development has shifted from "figure out the whole solution up front" to "build the smallest valuable piece, verify it, then build the next piece." Working through the SNHU Travel project sprint by sprint showed me how much risk that reduces: instead of discovering a misunderstanding after months of work, problems surface within a sprint or two, while they're still cheap to fix. Looking ahead, a few Agile practices I want to carry into my own work are Sprint Planning paired with a genuinely prioritized backlog (so I'm always working on the highest-value item, not just the most interesting one), continuous testing within each work cycle rather than saving it for the end, and regular retrospectives, even informally, to actually act on what went wrong instead of just noticing it. I also want to hold onto the habit of writing narrow, specific clarifying questions instead of vague ones; I saw firsthand in this course how much faster a team moves when a question can be answered in one sentence instead of requiring a meeting.

### What does it mean to be a good team member in software development?

Based on this course, being a good team member means making your own work visible and predictable so the rest of the team isn't blocked waiting on you, and being just as responsive when someone else is blocked waiting on you. That showed up concretely in the tester and developer communication samples from my Sprint Review: the most effective emails I wrote or received weren't the longest ones, they were the ones that asked or answered something specific enough to act on immediately. I also learned that being a good team member sometimes means pushing back constructively, like advocating for lighter-weight test documentation instead of carrying over heavyweight Waterfall practices, and being able to justify that position to the team rather than just agreeing to avoid conflict. Above all, the case studies we reviewed this term (Vision Quest, ChadaTech) reinforced that isolated "specialist silos" are the enemy of a healthy team; being a good teammate means staying visible, sharing knowledge, and treating the team's success as more important than individual ownership of a piece of code.
